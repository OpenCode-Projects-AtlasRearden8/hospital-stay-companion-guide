# The Bill-Review Step

Weeks after the goodbyes, the mail starts arriving: statements,
explanations, windowed envelopes with quiet fonts. This final chapter
is a bridge first and a mini-review second — the full treatment of
bills lives in the **medical-bills-comfort-guide**, this manual's
companion. Here we make sure the *hospital-stay-shaped* paperwork
gets reviewed by your own calm eyes, on your own kitchen table, in
your own time.

> **The calm note that opens the bills guide and opens this one too:**
> *nothing terrible happens if you take two weeks to review medical
> paperwork.* Late-reviewing is not late-paying in most situations,
> most providers accept plans far beyond statements' drama, and the
> errors you'll catch are much more common than the panic is useful.

## What lands in the mailbox after a stay (the four envelopes)

| Envelope | What it says | What you do with it (here) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hospital statement | "Here's what we're billing: room, supplies, the procedure" | Set it next to the insurer's version (below) and reconcile |
| EOB (Explanation of Benefits) | The *insurer's* paper about the same days: what was billed, what was "discounted"/adjusted, what they'll pay, what it says you may owe | The comparison tool — see below |
| Pharmacy statements | New prescriptions, some surprising at first | Simple check vs. the discharge med list |
| Equipment/ancillary bills | The walker, the DME vendor, sometimes an unrecognized lab name | Chapter 5's "ordered vs. merely listed" makes this legible |

The whole framework for what these envelopes mean and how to dispute
them lives in the bills guide (its chapters 1–3): the EOB-vs-bill
comparison, the deductible arithmetic with round numbers, and the
"Big Seven" checks. **We don't repeat that material here** — we just
give the hospital-stay version of two checks that matter most for
stays.

## Check 1: the dates-match check (the stay's version)

Any stay generates many sites, dates, and providers that were never in
the room together. So: **read the EOB line items next to the calendar
itself.** The admission date, the discharge date, and every date
in-between that has a charge — round each one down to "was someone
connected to her actual care in the building that day?" The answer is
usually yes with a name attached — one you then match against the
discharge paperwork (the folder usually names the main doctors and
visit days plainly). The classic stumbles here are everyone's
classic: dates that straddle midnight (admitted at 11 p.m. on the
4th), status words like "observation" being counted differently
than you'd expect, and separate facility-versus-doctor ("hospital"
and "physician") lines for the same visit that both *refer to the
same day* — uncommon, but a valid question to bring to a billing
person.

## Check 2: the providers-match check

The stay paperwork lists its main cast: the facility, the attendings,
the specialists, the lab that got a specimen or two, the equipment
vendor. Your EOBs and statements name billers. **Match the cast to
the credits** — anyone billing your days whose *name isn't
connected to any other paper you were handed* is your natural
question, and billing offices are on your side for exactly this
category: "Can you tell me who this provider was, and what that
charge was for?" is a complete sentence in every state.

## The one hospital question that's worth its own visit

Call the hospital's billing number (you grabbed it at admission in
chapter 2, or it's on any statement's corner; either path works) and
ask, politely and in this order:

1. **"Can I have an itemized bill?"** — itemized bills tell you what
   the statement was built from (it's a standard request, free, and
   it takes about five minutes to fulfill).
2. **"Does this include any charges for things that were cancelled
   or not done?"** — you'd be asking because you have the discharge
   paperwork's own account of what was done. Your calendar and
   folder give you the receipts.
3. **"What financial-assistance programs exist here, and what are
   the qualifications?"** — mid-recovery is fine to ask; care is
   never affected, and non-profit hospitals *carry* such programs as
   part of their normal operations. The bills guide's chapter 5 has
   the full questions list; this is just planting the flag.

Then, if anything looks like a genuine error, the bills guide takes
over: its dispute-letter templates, its claims log worksheet, its
phone scripts. Your role in *this* guide is simply to flag the
unfamiliar names and dates; its worksheets give them a clean ledger
to live in.

## The stay's three-minute review ritual

Before you file any of it in the box that is the *system*, sit down
once with the stack and three colors of pen:

- **Green (check):** every line item you recognized.
- **Yellow (verify):** anything unfamiliar — count them. One or two
  unfamiliar lines are usually settled by one phone call; a paragraph
  of them makes the itemized bill the starring attraction.
- **Red (decide):** anything marked as *patient responsibility* —
  the amount the insurer's paperwork says you may owe — which *you*
  now decide to pay after the checks above, or to call about.

This is one person's version of a claims audit — the bills guide's
full workbook version has columns, dates, and a running log; use
whichever fits your week's shape. And when the stack is filed, the
hospital week quietly becomes a chapter that had paperwork in it.

*Education only — never medical advice, and never legal or financial
advice either. This chapter is paperwork reading, and the
professionals (billing lines, the pharmacy, the insurer's member
services) are the ones with answers whenever a question gets
specific.*
