# Teach-Back: Questions for Admit and Discharge

Here's a polite superpower with a dry name. **Teach-back** is a
communication habit borrowed from the best patient-education practice:
instead of asking "does that make sense?" (the human race answers yes,
entirely on autopilot), you play back what you heard *in your own words*,
and let the professional correct you.

> "Just so I'm sure I heard this right — you're saying the new pills
> replace the blue one completely, and the only side effect to call
> about right away is a rash. Did I get that right?"

Two beautiful things happen. First, mistakes in your playback get
corrected *while the teacher is standing there* — which is the cheapest
possible moment to fix them. Second, the teacher gets useful information
(*the part that needed three repeats yesterday is still fuzzy*) and, from
sheer professional joy, explains the rest even better.

This is an education about conversations, not medicine: **teach-back is
how you make sure the words reach you fully intact. What to do is
always the care team's call.**

## The universal opener (memorize this, ship it everywhere)

- "Just to make sure I understand — I want to play back what you just
  said. You're saying… [your words]. Is that right?"
- Variant for the tired: "Could I play it back to you? My memory's
  asking for a re-run today."

Nobody is ever offended. In fact, nurses are the profession most
fiercely trained to *love* this move — it's the habit their training
recommends for exactly the moments you're worried about.

## At admission — four playback questions

The first day is measured in decisions arriving in batches. These four
catch the ones that matter most:

1. **The plan-for-today playback.** "Quick playback of this morning's
   plan so I've got it: the tests are in the morning, nothing happens
   until then, and we'll hear more after those results. Right?"
2. **Who's in charge.** "Walk me through it once more: who is the
   person running the overall plan this week, and who do we ask when
   that person isn't here?" (The titles exist in the [glossary](08-glossary.md);
   the playback makes them real.)
3. **What changes and what doesn't.** "So nothing about the evening
   routine changed today — she eats, sleeps, and takes her usual meds
   exactly as normal until the team says otherwise. Correct?"
4. **The family-loop check.** "And we're clear that *both* me and my
   brother are on the have-discussions-with list, plus you'd call his
   phone first if there's an immediate question tonight?"

## At discharge — the teach-back seven

Discharge instructions arrive famously fast, in a folder that mutters.
Before you hit the elevator (or as close to it as the schedule allows),
run these seven playbacks. They map directly onto chapter 5's
discharge checklist, where each gets its own deep-dive.

1. **Meds.** "Playback of the medicines: this list replaces what she
   takes at home — the new blood-pressure pill happens twice a day
   *with food*, the antibiotics finish their full ten days no matter
   what, and the entire pharmacy-side med *list* — every vitamin and
   supplement too — is what she saw at admission. What changed is
   [*…*]."
2. **The care-at-home part.** "We can do everything for wound care
   without special training — but the *first time* we'd like a nurse
   from home health to show us in person. That's normal to ask for?"
   (Asking *is* normal; the answer goes in the discharge checklist.)
3. **Warning signs.** "So the signs that say *call today, don't
   overnight it* are: fever over [*their number*], the wound looking
   red and puffy and spreading, and any new dizziness on standing.
   Playback correct?"
4. **The restriction list.** "No driving for two weeks, no lifting
   over ten pounds — a gallon of milk — for four, and stairs are fine
   but slow and near-the-railing."
5. **Follow-ups.** "The appointments: with Dr. [*name*] in about ten
   days, and whoever they recommended to call for booking — I've got
   that number. Both written here and in my phone." (Booking is *our*
   job, not a value judgment; chapter 5 says who books what.)
6. **The call-numbers page.** "Two numbers on the last page: the one
   for weekdays, and the one for nights and weekends where we'll reach
   whoever's on call. We don't have to be sure something's wrong to
   call an on-call person — being unsure is itself on the list."
7. **The one-thing check.** "In one sentence — what's the one
   thing you most want us not to miss this week?" (You'll get the
   truest answer of the day on this one.)

## Teach-back when the patient is the one learning

Helpers don't only learn. Patients are handed instructions about their
own recovery — and a patient can teach-back too ("let me say it back —
you want me walking three times a day, even just around the room?").
The helper's role with a nervous patient is playing the tape recorder:

- "Mr. Ruiz, you had it right: three walks a day. Tell you what, I'll
  fold it into my notes too."
- If the playback reveals a gap ("wait, medicine *before* food?"),
  hand the moment to the professional — the *question* is ours, the
  clarification is theirs. That's teamwork, not overstepping.

## If you feel embarrassed using it

You are performing the single behavior hospitals' own patient-education
materials *ask* you to perform. You're not cross-examining anybody;
you're double-checking a handoff between two people using different
vocabularies — the oldest coordination problem in the world. The
printed card in [cards/teach-back-card.md](cards/teach-back-card.md)
exists so you can literally rehearse in the hallway first. And there's
a version of embarrassment that's more expensive: the 11 p.m. call
with "wait, *when* do the new pills start?"

*Education only, never medical advice — this chapter teaches ways to
talk with each other, not what medicine or care decisions to make.*
