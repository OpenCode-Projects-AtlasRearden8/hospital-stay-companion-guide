# The Hospital Stay Companion Guide

## Start Here

A hospital stay is one of those weeks where everything feels loud at once.
The plan was a scheduled procedure, or it wasn't a plan at all — it was an
emergency room at 2 a.m. Either way, you're suddenly being asked to sign
things, remember things, and help someone you love, all while running on
bad coffee.

Take a breath. This manual is the calm friend at your elbow.

It is a **plain-English, education-only guide to the *practical* side of a
hospital stay**: what to pack and what to leave home, what those admission
forms actually say, how to be genuinely useful at the bedside (without
hovering), the questions worth asking at admission and discharge, how to
decode the discharge checklist, how to set the house up for homecoming,
and — at the very end — the bill-review step, so paperwork doesn't ambush
you a month later.

**First, the one promise this guide makes everywhere it can:** this is
education only, and **it is never medical advice**. Nothing here tells you
anything about care, diagnoses, medication choices, or treatment decisions.
For anything about your actual health, your nurse is the best teacher you
will ever have — that's literally their training — and your doctor is the
person to ask about decisions. This guide only helps with the logistics
around the edges.

## Who this is for

- **The patient** who wants to feel less like paperwork is happening *to* them.
- **The bedside person** — spouse, adult child, sibling, neighbor — who
  wants to actually help this week, not just sit in a corner.
- Anyone who has ever been handed a discharge stack of papers, nodded
  politely, and thought *"what did I just agree to?"*

You don't need to be good at hospitals, paperwork, or medical vocabulary.
Every chapter explains terms in plain words the first time (and the
[Glossary](08-glossary.md) collects them all).

## How to use this guide

There are two paths, and you may take both:

**The planned stay.** If the surgery or procedure is already on the
calendar, read [What to Pack and What to Leave Home](01-packing.md) the
week before, then skim [Admission Paperwork in Plain Words](02-admission-paperwork.md)
so the clipboard holds no surprises. Pack once, sleep fine.

**The surprise stay.** If you got here by ambulance and a cousin is
grabbing the overnight bag, go straight to the
[Packing Card](cards/packing-card.md) — one printable page, text them the
link. Then read [Teach-Back: Questions for Admit and Discharge](04-teach-back.md)
whenever your brain wakes up.

**Either way**, before discharge day arrives, make time for:
[The Discharge Checklist, Decoded](05-discharge-checklist.md) — it unpicks
the meds list, the follow-up appointments, the equipment, and the
*who-to-call* page — and [Homecoming Setup](06-homecoming.md). Then a few
weeks later, when the envelopes start arriving:
[The Bill-Review Step](07-bill-review.md).

## The short version

1. **Pack light, pack smart.** ID, insurance card, med list, glasses,
   phone charger. Almost everything else is optional.
2. **The forms say simpler things than their titles suggest.** We'll
   translate the consent forms and the privacy ones, line by line.
3. **Bring your medications *list*, not the pills themselves** — and
   confirm what the care team wants you to do, because different
   hospitals want different things.
4. **A good bedside helper is a journalist and a butler, not a doctor.**
   Take notes, run errands, ferry questions. Care decisions stay with
   the care team.
5. **Teach-back is the polite superpower.** "Just to make sure I heard
   you right, you're saying…" catches miscommunication without arguing.
6. **The discharge checklist is a puzzle where every piece has a
   question worth asking.** Meds changed? Follow-ups booked? Equipment
   delivered? Who do we call at 9 p.m.?
7. **Set the house up on the easy day**, not the exhausted day.
8. **Bills wait for you.** Nothing terrible happens if you take two
   weeks to review them — and reviewing them catches real errors.

## Two small but important rules of the road

- **We translate paperwork; we never interpret it.** If a consent form
  mentions a risk you don't understand, that is a question for the care
  team, out loud, before signing. This guide tells you how to ask.
- **We handle logistics, never medicine.** The moment a topic about
  treatment appears — whether it's a symptom, a drug, or a decision —
  this guide hands you back to the professionals and shows you how to
  get your question into words.
