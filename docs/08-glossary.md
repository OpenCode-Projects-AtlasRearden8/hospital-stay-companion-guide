# Glossary

Plain-words definitions for the words that show up around a hospital
stay. This is an *educational vocabulary* glossary, not a clinical one —
it explains what a term means on paper and in hallways, never what it
means for any particular person's care. Where a term is medical, the
definition ends the same way every clinical question should: that one
goes to your care team.

## The forms and folders

- **Admission** — the process of becoming an official inpatient:
  registering, the folder of forms (chapter 2 decodes it), and the
  bed.
- **Discharge** — the process of going home, plus the folder of
  instructions you get on the way out. "Discharge paperwork" is the
  stack this guide decodes in chapter 5.
- **Consent to treat** — the signature that lets the hospital provide
  general care. Different from consent for a *specific procedure*,
  which comes with its own risks-and-alternatives conversation.
- **HIPAA** — the federal privacy law. Practically: it sets who your
  information can and cannot be shared with, and gives you rights
  over it. The "who may we talk to" form maps it onto your family.
- **Advance directive / living will / healthcare proxy** — papers
  recording a person's wishes and decision-makers for situations
  where they can't speak for themselves. Hospitals ask about them as
  a routine checkbox; having one is a personal/financial/legal
  matter, usually with a lawyer or a state's free forms — never
  something this top of guide instructs on beyond "they exist and
  the question is normal."
- **Patient advocate / case manager** — hospital staff who help with
  the non-medical logistics: discharge planning, equipment
  arrangements, insurance paperwork, billing questions. Free to ask
  for at any large hospital.
- **Social worker (hospital)** — the discharge-planning professional;
  home health arrangements, equipment orders, post-hospital services
  are their daily choreography.
- **Prior authorization** — when an insurer wants to pre-approve a
  planned service before it happens. Administrative gate, not a
  medical one; your provider's office usually runs it.

## The people

- **Attending physician** — the doctor in overall charge of the
  patient's care during the stay. When the big questions come up,
  this is whose team handles them.
- **Nurse, RN / LPN** — the around-the-clock professionals; *your*
  best teachers for anything the patient is going through, per
  everything this guide has said about asking questions out loud.
- **Charge nurse** — the shift's floor manager; the "friendly
  escalation" for administrative issues like room problems or
  schedule fairness.
- **PCP (primary care physician/provider)** — the home-base doctor,
  who usually sees the patient after discharge; often the
  *follow-up to book* in chapter 5's checklist.
- **Specialist** — a doctor focused on one area of medicine.
- **On call** — the doctor covering calls when yours isn't
  personally available (nights, weekends). Answering services route
  to them; the "call page" in the discharge folder names the routes.
- **Home health agency** — the service that sends nurses or
  therapists to the house per the discharge orders; scheduling it is
  chapter 5 territory.
- **Caretaker/caregiver, bedside helper** — the family/friend role
  this guide's chapter 3 maps: journalist-and-butler, with
  permission.

## The words rooms grow

- **Rounds** — the daily team walk-through, when plans get updated
  out loud; the bedside-helper note-taking moment.
- **Inpatient / outpatient** — overnight formality (inpatient) vs.
  same-day (outpatient). Matters for billing and for "which papers
  you'll get."
- **Observation status** — an overnight-ish status that can affect
  billing even though it looks like a hospital night; worth knowing
  the word, and its money-questions go to the billing office
  (chapter 7's phone numbers).
- **Status post-op (s/p)** — paperwork shorthand for *after the
  operation*.
- **NPO** — *nothing by mouth*, meaning no food or drink for a
  while, as the care team directs (surgery mornings famously).
- **IV** — the drip line. Universal, boring, and nothing to be
  afraid of; the nurses put them in all day.
- **Vitals** — the regular measurements (temperature, pulse, blood
  pressure, breathing) taken throughout the day.
- **Meds / the med list** — medications. The *list* is the crown
  document of chapter 5: what's new, changed, stopped, unchanged.

## The paperwork after

- **EOB (Explanation of Benefits)** — the *insurer's* letter about a
  claim: billed, adjusted, paid, "patient responsibility." Compare it
  against the provider's bill before paying anything (bills guide,
  chapter 1, is the full house).
- **Itemized bill** — the provider's line-by-line bill; free to
  request, worth requesting whenever a statement is confusing.
- **Patient responsibility** — the portion the insurer's paperwork
  says may be owed by the patient. Round numbers live in the bills
  guide; the review ritual is chapter 7 here.
- **DME (durable medical equipment)** — walkers, shower chairs,
  ramps-that-aren't-quite: the equipment line items of chapters 5
  and 6.
- **Home health visit** — the agency's nurse/therapist coming to
  the house per discharge orders; confirm whether someone booked it
  (see the "who books whom" habit).
- **Financial assistance / charity care** — programs some hospitals
  (especially non-profits) run for bills; asking about them is
  normal, free, and never affects care.

## Two words this guide declines to translate into instruction

- **Symptoms** — anywhere in the hospital, home, or mailbox: what a
  symptom means or what to do about one is a care-team question,
  through the call page and the professionals it names. The word
  "symptoms" is simply the *reason to call*.
- **Prognosis** — a conversation for the people with the chart;
  "what does this mean going forward?" is answered by them, and
  this guide is glad to help you ask it well (chapter 4's sentences).

**A closing translation note:** every term above is defined so you
can *recognize it and ask about it*. Definitions in this glossary are
vocabulary, not instructions. Vocabulary is the toolkit; the
professionals carry the tools into use.
