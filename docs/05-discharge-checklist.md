# The Discharge Checklist, Decoded

Discharge day arrives with a folder, a rolling cart, and a
sentences-a-minute nurse who has been doing this beautifully since
before you got there. The folder is thoroughly good — hospitals improve
it every year — but it's still a folder full of *parts*. This chapter
takes it apart on the kitchen table (figuratively; you'll get the real
one on the bed) and names each part, plus the one question that
unlocks it.

First, the shape of the thing. A typical discharge packet contains five
kinds of paper, and knowing the shape makes every subsequent page feel
like something you expected to meet:

1. **Instructions** (how to care for the person at home — wound care,
   activity limits, diet notes — written for the patient's *home*
   life),
2. **The medication list** (the new official one — see below),
3. **Follow-up information** (appointments to be booked, by whom),
4. **The "when to call" page** (numbers and the warning signs the
   team wants you to know — these come *from* the team; our job is
   only to find them, read them aloud, and store them where 11 p.m.
   can reach them),
5. **Administrative loose ends** (home health arrangements requested,
   equipment vendors ordered, restriction notes for employers or
   schools, and the billing contact info chapter 7 will want).

Run the [teach-back seven](04-teach-back.md) before you leave; this
chapter tells you *what to look at* afterwards, when the folder meets
the kitchen table.

## Part 1 — The medication list (the one that matters most)

One list becomes law today: the discharge med list. The question to
hold over *every other piece of medication paper in the house* is
simply: **"which list is current?"**

- **New prescriptions** added — with doses, times, and for how long
  ("the antibiotic — ten days, all of them, no skipped ones; the
  course matters more than the comfort of quitting early, says the
  paper, and that's a pharmacy-and-provider conversation to accept
  whole).
- **Old home meds stopped, changed, or kept** — the discharge list
  usually says which. Mark them into three buckets: *new*, *changed*,
  *unchanged*. Anything "stopped" gets physically removed from the
  medicine cabinet today (safely — many pharmacies take old meds back;
  this is administrative housekeeping, not dosing advice).
- **Supplements and vitamins** — they belong on the list or not as the
  team says; just make the list itself honest about reality.
- **The pharmacist is the free, undervalued ally here.** One visit
  ("we came home with four new medicines on this sheet — can we walk
  through the whole list as it now stands?") is modest, priceless
  education, and exactly what pharmacists are trained to do. Bring
  the discharge sheet, every bottle from the house, and a pen.

The one-evergreen organizational tip in this whole manual: **one
master list, in one place, in one pen.** Phone photo backups welcome.

## Part 2 — Follow-ups: the part where *you* are the project manager

Here's the open secret of discharge folders: **"Follow-up: 2 weeks,
PCP" is an instruction, but the *booking* is unsupervised homework.**
Nobody will call first in most cases — you book. It fails not because
families are careless but because care + recovery is a lot; routinize
it instead:

- Tonight: every follow-up in the folder goes onto **one list** with
  three columns — *who, by-when, booked?*
- Tomorrow morning (the natural moment: energy is highest and kit is
  assembled): make the calls.
  - The PCP/primary: usually you (or the front desk) book this
    yourself — say the words "recent hospital discharge" when
    booking; providers are trained to move these up the list.
  - The hospital's specialty clinic: often they self-book check-back
    visits, but *confirm* it on the phone being the rule of
    least-drama. "Did you happen to book it, or do we call
    someone?" is a real and normal question.
- Any **third-party orders** (physical therapy, home nursing visits,
  labs) usually arrive attached to a referral — record who was
  supposed to call *us*, so 48 hours of silence becomes a problem
  with a phone number, not a worry with a shrug.

## Part 3 — Equipment and supplies: what's listed, what's ordered

Discharge folders mention equipment variously: "patient may need
walker status post-op" ("status post-op" = after the operation —
[the glossary](08-glossary.md) collects these). The decoded checklist:

- **What did they *order*, versus what did they merely *list*?**
  Ordered items usually arrive with a vendor's name and a delivery
  window in the paperwork (or a social worker's note in the margin).
  Listed-but-not-ordered items — the humble "as needed: shower
  chair" — quietly become our task, and where
  [Homecoming Setup](06-homecoming.md) picks the thread up.
- **Home health/nursing orders:** if "home health requested" appears,
  they'll contact you to schedule the first visit; if nobody has
  called by the day after discharge, the *call is allowed to go the
  other way* — the folder's admin section usually names the agency.
- **Supplies list** (dressing materials and the like): pharmacies
  with a medical-supplies counter often carry all of it in one stop,
  and pharmacists teach dressing technique for *your specific
  products* when asked. (Demonstrations of care technique belong to
  pros — the pharmacist teaching *the product use* is squarely
  education, free, and excellent. See the not-medical-advice rule
  being helpful again.)

## Part 4 — The call page: quotes from the "when to call" sheet

This rare page is the crown jewel of the folder, and it deserves
premium handling:

- **It has two phone numbers, minimum** — the daytime clinic line and
  the nights-and-weekends line, where an answering service connects
  you to whoever is on call. Both go on the fridge (yes, really —
  this is the one piece of documentation this guide happily endorses
  putting in a place that loud).
- **It contains a *list* of symptoms the team wants you to call
  about** — that list is the team's, in the team's words; copy it
  verbatim into your master note, then let the number's on-call
  professionals do their trained jobs if it ever comes up. You do
  the calling; they do the medicine. No exceptions, by design.
- **When unsure, call and describe.** "I'm not sure which side of
  the line this is on" describes a real situation that on-call teams
  field all the time; it's a complete sentence at 11 p.m. and it is
  *never* an annoyance. Patients' families overestimate the
  annoyance and underestimate the grace about forty-fold, per
  centuries of precedent.

## Part 5 — The admin tail (three minutes, saves weeks)

- **Billing contact info**: put the hospital's billing number and the
  insurer's member-services number into chapter 7's future reuse.
- **Work/school paperwork**: if the folder mentions restrictions or
  avoidances for work, the note is what you give an employer or
  school; HR types want the paper they can file, and the clinics can
  usually supply extra copies for free at 2 a.m. of asking.
- **Insurance paperwork flags**: some hospitalizations turn on prior
  authorizations or referral paperwork (elective things do, always);
  note in the master list anything marked "before next visit, call
  insurance." The *free-health-admin-kit* companion handles the
  general insurance paperwork choreography; the bills guide handles
  the after-this-bill.

## The completed checklist (print me)

A one-page recap exists here for exactly the moment the folder feels
tall: [cards/packing-card.md](cards/packing-card.md) covers bags; the
discharge version lives in the teach-back card, and this section is
its text companion. The short form:

- [ ] Med list: new / changed / stopped / unchanged — all four
      bucketed; cabinet updated; master list updated
- [ ] Pharmacy walkthrough booked (bring list + all bottles)
- [ ] Follow-ups: list made, calls started, "who books whom"
      recorded
- [ ] Equipment: ordered vs. merely listed, both checked
- [ ] Home health: appear or chase (phone number found either way)
- [ ] Call page: both numbers on the fridge; symptom list copied
      verbatim (their words, not ours)
- [ ] Billing/admin numbers into the master note for later
- [ ] One master note exists, and the whole household knows where

This is the chapter that mostly rewards the boring virtue of *writing
things down in one place*. The guide's next stop —
[Homecoming Setup](06-homecoming.md) — is the fun one, where the
parts you've collected become one welcoming week.

*Education only, never medical advice: meds, symptoms, and care
instructions are read back to the care team and the pharmacy — never
decided here.*
