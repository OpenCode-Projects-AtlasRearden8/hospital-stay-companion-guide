# How to Be a Genuinely Useful Bedside Helper

You've cleared your calendar, stocked the vending machine, and taken a
seat in the stiffest chair in America. Now what?

Here's the honest framing: a bedside helper's job is not to be a second
nurse, a doctor, or a superhero. The job is **journalist and butler** —
with the patient's permission, which is the sentence most people skip.

> **Rule zero:** ask the patient what they want. Some patients want a
> constant companion; some want quiet hours and a person on deck. Ask
> early (in the hall, not the bedside, when you can), ask without
> judgment, and revisit because the answer changes with the week.

## The journalist half

The helper's most valuable artifact this week is **one place where the
stay's story is written down**. A notebook or a phone note is enough.

- **Morning rounds, one big paragraph.** When the team sweeps
  through — note who they were (the white coat is often the team's
  explaining member), what they said the plan for today is, and
  anything you can't run down later.
- **Who is who.** Patient-facing teams rotate daily; nobody expects you
  to memorize names. Listing "Sam — day nurse Mon" prevents ten small
  cases of mistaken identity.
- **The running questions list.** Two columns: "asked, answered" and
  "still open." The open column is your ticket to the next
  conversation, which is naturally a teaching moment for the nurse of
  the moment to fill in with plain words (it's actually their daily
  work, and good notes make it better).
- **The numbers that recur.** They'll mention dates for follow-ups and
  a count of things ordered — capturing five words then saves a
  frantic text-chain later.

## The butler half

Small logistics, done quietly, in order of how much they'll matter:

1. **Answer the phone-tree for the patient.** Calling Aunt Jean and the
   coworker who texts "any news?" every hour so the patient doesn't have
   to answer everything at 3% battery — deeply underrated kindness.
2. **Keep the room charging-friendly.** Cables untangled, the one
   true cord guarded with your life, glasses found *before* they're
   wanted.
3. **The hallway option.** Some questions belong with the care team,
   but not at the patient's bedside. When you have one of those,
   tell the charge nurse: "Is there a moment I could ask a couple of
   small questions without him having to respond?" Standard practice,
   and welcome — the hallway conversation exists.
4. **Food runs that respect the medical side.** Ask before bringing
   *any* food or drink for the patient — teams often have exactly
   specified rules (which is a care-team answer, not a helper guess).
   But you are always allowed one for yourself.
5. **The home front as a single ask.** Create one group text: "Here's
   what's happening today; here's what's needed." Widely distributed
   sporadic duties beat one person with a martyr complex.
6. **Ferrying the discharge-forward intel.** Starting in the middle of
   the stay, the discharge chapter (5) needs three small things
   collected along the way: which equipment might follow us home, who
   the follow-up appointments will be with, and any paper that says
   "call us if…". Helpers who collect these mid-stay sleep better.

## The hard stops (being useful means *not* doing these)

- **Don't diagnose, don't web-search for the room, don't debate the
  plan.** Care decisions are for the care team. Bringing the patient
  their own *questions* (the teach-back chapter) is helper work;
  bringing the *answers from a forum* is not.
- **Don't negotiate at the bedside with the person who's tired.**
  Long hallway (or better, the family room) for anything that could
  become a disagreement about rules: visiting hours, food, sleeping
  arrangements.
- **Don't app vlog the stay.** Photos are for private family
  sentiment, never posts. Same reasoning as the guide everywhere:
  privacy is a thing people don't know they regret giving up until
  they do — and hospital rooms are other people's stories too (the
  roommate's say-so obviously counts).
- **Report, don't interpret.** If something's off — say the pump is
  beeping oddly, or someone seems more confused than this morning —
  the *question* "could you take a look at that?" is the tool. The
  nurse's clinical call is the nurse's. Our job is the report, not
  the interpretation. (Education only; never medical advice.)

## A gentle note about *you*

Helpers famously forget that they are also a body. Eat at normal
hours; sleep at least one full night of your week this week; make the
walks. The stay is a marathon with surprise weeks attached. The most
useful helper in the building is the one still standing on day five —
which is one of the many reasons the patient will *also* nag you, and
this guide is glad to relay it.

*Education only, never medical advice: care questions go through the
care team always — this chapter is logistics, kindness, and good notes.*
