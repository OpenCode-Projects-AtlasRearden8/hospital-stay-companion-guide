# What to Pack and What to Leave Home

You are packing for a stay nobody would choose, in a building where someone
else does the cleaning and the meals. That's the good news: **you need far
less than your anxiety says you do**. A small bag that fits under a
chair beats a suitcase nobody can maneuver.

Whether the stay was on the calendar for six weeks or it started in an
emergency room, the packing list is nearly the same shape — the difference
is who does the packing (you calmly, or a scout sent from your couch).

> **Before anything else:** one rule from this guide that matters most.
> **Bring your medications *list*. Whether to bring the actual pill
> bottles varies by hospital**, so ask. The admitting nurse will tell you
> in one sentence what they want — some want the bottles brought in and
> sent home with family, some want them left home. There is no wrong
> answer to the question itself; the wrong move is guessing.

## The core list (fits in one small bag)

- **ID and insurance card.** Photo ID, insurance card, and any pharmacy
  discount cards. Wallet stays with the patient or goes home with the
  bedside person — not loose on a bedside table (hospitals advise
  sending valuables home; that's their standard advice, and this guide
  agrees in spirit).
- **The medications list.** Name of each drug, dose, how often, and
  prescribing doctor. Everyone who helps you should be able to find
  this in ten seconds (many people keep a photo of it on their phone).
- **Glasses, hearing aids, and their cases.** Readers count. So do
  hearing-aid batteries and, if dentures are part of your life, a
  denture cup with a name written on it. Losing these items early in a
  stay is a cliché; the cases and the labels prevent the cliché.
- **Phone + charger + a long cable.** Bed outlets are never where
  physics suggests. A 10-foot cable earns its space.
- **Personal hygiene basics.** Toothbrush, toothpaste, deodorant,
  comb, lip balm, lotion. The hospital has some of this; your own
  version feels like your own life.
- **One piece of home.** A photo, a small comfort object, a book you
  keep meaning to read. Something that smells like ordinary.
- **Loose, comfortable clothing to go *home* in.** The hospital
  supplies the gown; you supply the exit outfit (front-fastening tops
  are kinder to post-procedure mornings than pullovers — a practical
  note, not a medical one).
- **Contact names + phone numbers on paper.** Two or three people, and
  which one is the main contact. Batteries die; paper doesn't.

## Worth considering

- A small notebook or a running note on the phone — you'll want to
  write down things people tell you (see the bedside and teach-back
  chapters for why).
- Earplugs or an eye mask. Hospitals are bright, loud, and interrupted.
- A list of allergies and past reactions, written down plainly.
- Insurance-company contact info and any prior-authorization numbers,
  if a procedure was arranged in advance.
- For a longer stay: changes of the loosest clothing (drawstring
  waistbands have saved many mornings) and non-slip socks with grippy
  soles, if your hospital doesn't supply them.
- Coins/bills for vending machines — small amounts, because nothing
  valuable travels here.

## What to leave at home

- **Valuables and most jewelry.** Rings other than a wedding band tend
  to cause more drama than comfort. Hospitals ask you to send valuables
  home with family; this is one place where this guide happily echoes
  the hospitals.
- **Large sums of cash and all the credit cards except one.** A patient
  account for small purchases exists at most hospitals; one card is
  plenty.
- **The whole medicine cabinet.** Not the bottles — and not ten
  supplements, either. The *list* covers it (supplements, vitamins, and
  herbal things belong on that list too; the care team will decide what
  matters, and they want the full truth).
- **The family computer, the entire photo album, anything you'd truly
  grieve.** The bedside is a busy, spilled-water, borrowed-charger kind
  of place.
- **Alcohol and outside medications without a green light** — ask
  before bringing anything ingestible into the building. This one is
  about safety as well as rules, which is why the guide is happy to
  defer to the care team on it.

## For a planned stay: the pre-packing evening

1. Follow the hospital's own prep instructions first — diet, arrival
   time, what to have ready. Those come from your specific care team,
   and they beat every generic list, including this one.
2. Pack the core list the night *before* the night before. Then sleep on
   the question "what's missing?" — mornings invent needs; evenings
   know the truth.
3. Photocopy or photograph both sides of the insurance card.
4. Charge the phone, write the contact page, put the list in a zip
   pocket of the bag.
5. Tell your main contact person: hospital name and floor/room if
   known, arrival time, and their one job (see the bedside chapter).

## For a surprise stay: the scout list

Someone is about to be dispatched from your kitchen table. The
[Packing Card](cards/packing-card.md) condenses this chapter into one
printable page designed for exactly that courier. Their mission:

- get the core list above into a small bag,
- include the medications list (photo on a phone is fine),
- bring glasses/hearing aids, a charger, and comfortable clothes for later,
- and — quietly important — pack *enough for the scout too* (they may
  be sleeping on the couch down the hall): water bottle, phone
  charger, spare layer.

> Packing for hospitals rewards minimalism: the caretakers supply the
> linens, the meals, and the social workers; the bag supplies the
> *person* — their glasses, their comforts, and their lifeline home.

*Education only — never medical advice. Anything shifted for health
reasons (what to bring, when to arrive, what to leave) is the care
team's call; this list is logistics around their answers.*
