# Homecoming Setup

The drive home is a triumph. The first hour home is a plot twist: the
person is home, the house is unchanged, and the energy levels are not.
This chapter is about making the house itself into a helper instead of
an obstacle course — do it on the *easy* day (if there was one, e.g.,
a planned stay with a heads-up weekend), or spend the first home
morning doing the short version.

One framing idea up front: homecoming setup is **interior design with
an agenda** — fewer things to reach, fewer stairs to think about
thinking about, fewer "has anyone seen my…" moments. None of this is
medical advice about what a body *should* tolerate; it's comfort
engineering around whatever the care team's activity instructions already
say.

## The first-night plan (do this the day before, if you can)

- **The landing zone.** One main floor if there are two floors: a
  comfortable chair-with-armrests (armrests do real work for sitting
  down), a side table with water, pills-on-schedule, phone charger,
  glasses, and a stack of reading material chosen for low stakes.
- **The duplicates basket.** Travel toothbrush and other
  essentials, a change of loose clothes downstairs — so the stairs
  get crossed once a day on purpose rather than eight times while
  hunting.
- **Meals: the kindness fridge.** Five ready things — soup, the
  scrambled-egg ingredients, noodles — labeled and at eye level. This
  isn't meal preparation ambition; it's removal of the 5 p.m. tax.
- **The first-night list** on a notecard: meds time, the morning's
  goals (mostly: rest), the call-numbers page taped somewhere visible
  for everyone's calm.
- **Sleep arrangements** as instructed by the team (elevation,
  specific positions, etc. — all *their* answers, per the folder),
  managed by *you* at bedroom level: extra pillows found tonight,
  not found at 2 a.m.

## The housekeeping audit — thirty quiet minutes

Walk the normal daily path (bed → bathroom → kitchen → favorite
chair) with one question in your mouth: **what could make each
transition easier?** Watch for:

- **Rugs and cords and trip hazards.** The classic list, still true.
  Small rugs skip storage for the season; cables get zip-tied along
  walls. Also: lamps within reach from bed, so nobody walks anywhere
  in the dark.
- **The bathroom arrangement:** if the folder lists equipment like a
  shower chair or grab bars and they're not installed yet, that's
  tonight's *helper task* (installation is a hardware-and-a-sensible-
  sibling activity, not a skill issue) — plus the vendor deliveries
  from chapter 5 should schedule themselves here.
- **Kitchen geography:** the daily dishes live in the top
  cabinet-drawer layer now; the marble standing mixer takes a
  sabbatical. Nobody is running a cooking competition this week.
- **Doors and seats:** tight doorways, low chairs-without-armrests,
  the recliner that eats people — arrange or retire them for now.
  The armrest is the guide's recurring hero.

## The recovery week at household scale

- **One errand-runner exists.** Name the person whose job is
  pharmacy pickup, milk, and the post. Rides to the follow-up
  appointments also belong here, booked the evening before,
  *always* earlier than you'd think.
- **The visitors protocol with teeth:** short visits, and only when
  the patient says yes (ask fresh each time; the answer changes by
  the hour). "We're keeping visiting short for the first week —
  come at 4 and stay 20 minutes?" is a warm gate, not a cold one.
- **Chores, reduced and reassigned:** trash out, dishes run, laundry
  sorted-by-someone-else for the week. This is the week when the
  guide's *low-energy productivity* cousins would tell you to let
  good-enough be good enough, and they'd be correct.
- **The master note grows into the week's brain:** today's meds,
  tomorrow's appointment, next errand, next question for the
  follow-up visit. One page, everyone reads it.

## Wind-down planning (the part everyone forgets)

- **About day 3–4:** the helper's adrenaline fades right as the
  patient's ambitions rise. Plan something small for the patient to
  *host* — a cup of coffee at their own table counts — because
  hosting is identity, not chores.
- **The new-routine check-in:** about a week in, reread chapter 5's
  checklist together: follow-ups all booked? Meds list still the one
  law of the land? Anything on the "call us if" list never needed —
  and are the numbers still on the fridge anyway?
- **The thank-you round:** the neighbor who mowed, the friend who
  brought soup, the cousin who drove. Small acknowledgments keep the
  goodwill supply healthy for any future week (pre-stocking it *is*
  the whole strategy).

## When homecoming reveals a gap

Sometimes the house hums along and then a snag appears — the
equipment hasn't come, the follow-up date conflicts with a work trip,
the home health visit never got scheduled. The fix is always the
same: **the phone, to a named human, in daylight.** The discharge
folder's admin section numbers everything needed; if it doesn't, the
hospital's main-line operator will route you ("who handles home
health scheduling?"). Problems with *names and numbers* have a habit
of resolving.

*Education only, never medical advice — every instruction about
activity, care, and recovery belongs to the care team's folder and
follow-up visits; this chapter only arranges the furniture and the
calendar around it.*
